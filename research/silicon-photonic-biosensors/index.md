## Moving from molecular snapshots to molecular trajectories

Our bodies are constantly changing, and so are the molecular signals that reflect our health. Hormones fluctuate across menstrual cycles and life stages. Biomarkers associated with injury and disease can change as conditions progress or patients recover. Biological responses to therapies may also depend on physiological conditions that vary over time.

Yet many of our most powerful molecular measurement technologies remain concentrated in specialized laboratories. Although these methods provide valuable information, practical limitations on testing frequency and accessibility can make it difficult to capture how biological processes change over time.

How can we move from isolated molecular snapshots to a more complete understanding of molecular trajectories?

Our research aims to address this challenge by developing silicon photonic biosensors—miniature optical devices that use light to detect biological molecules.

## Using light to measure molecules

Silicon photonics allows light to be guided through microscopic structures fabricated on silicon chips using technologies related to semiconductor manufacturing.

By attaching molecular recognition elements, such as antibodies, to different sensing regions, we can design a single chip to detect multiple biological targets.

Our research combines these optical sensors with biochemical assays and microfluidic technologies, with the longer-term goal of creating compact systems capable of quantitative, multiplexed molecular measurements.

## Demonstrating multiplexed hormone detection

As an important proof of principle, our team has demonstrated the detection of two hormone-related biomarkers—follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and pregnanediol glucuronide (PdG)—on a single silicon photonic chip using artificial urine.

FSH is a protein hormone involved in reproductive function, while PdG is a urinary metabolite of progesterone. These molecules differ substantially in their biochemical properties and require different detection approaches.

Detecting both on one chip demonstrates the potential to combine different molecular assays within a shared sensing platform.

This work provides a foundation for developing more versatile molecular measurement systems.

## Where could better molecular measurements make a difference?

We are particularly interested in applications where access to molecular information is limited, where important biomarkers change over time, or where current measurement methods leave significant questions unanswered.

- **Women's health:** Investigating hormonal dynamics across menstrual cycles, perimenopause, and other physiological transitions.

- **Neurological health:** Developing assays for biomarkers associated with neural injury, with potential applications in studying neurological injury and recovery.

- **Therapeutic development:** Exploring how measurements of changing physiological conditions could help researchers investigate variability in responses to emerging therapies.

- **Health security and other applications:** Investigating adaptable molecular sensing approaches for infectious disease, distributed testing, and other settings where timely molecular information could be valuable.

## Our vision

Our longer-term goal is to make sophisticated molecular measurements more informative and accessible:

- **More biomarkers:** Measuring multiple molecular targets from the same sample.

- **More often:** Supporting repeated measurements to investigate changing biological processes.

- **More places:** Developing compact systems that could extend molecular testing beyond specialized laboratories.

Achieving this vision requires advances in molecular assays, microfluidics, integrated instrumentation, and application-specific validation.

Our research continues to advance these interconnected technologies, with the goal of enabling new ways to study changing biological processes and improve access to molecular information.
